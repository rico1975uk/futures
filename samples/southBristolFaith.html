In short

On the evidence available, St Mary Redcliffe and Temple School looks like the closest fit for Sam and your family’s priorities.

This is a decision aid, not a ranking of school quality. Match ratings are the tool’s independent view: published data seen through your child’s needs and your priorities. Your visit impressions are shown alongside, not counted in, so the two can be compared.

Before the detail

  • Most children settle. Even children who are anxious in July are usually settled by October half-term. The move is a bigger deal in prospect than in practice for most families.
  • Differences between schools are smaller than they look. Headline results mostly reflect who a school admits, and one year’s figures often drift back towards average the next. Your child’s own effort, friendships and support at home matter more to how they do.
  • No choice is irreversible. Children who are unhappy can and do move, and sixth form is another fresh start.
  • As Daniel Kahneman put it, “Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it.”

2. What the research suggests will help Sam

  1. Clear routines and help with getting organised. They does best with clear expectations and routines. Secondary school suddenly asks for a lot of self-management: many teachers, a timetable, homework platforms and equipment. Explicit routines, planners, and tutors who check in make a real difference while these skills are still developing.
  2. Plenty of people and activities to find their tribe. They get energy from people. A larger year group and a busy clubs programme give more chances to find friends and interests.

3. The move to Year 7

Likely to settle well. Your answers suggest few of the known risk factors for a difficult move to Year 7. The usual preparation (visiting, practising the journey, sorting kit) should be enough.

8. Questions to ask on every visit

Tailored to Sam. Ask the same questions everywhere so you can compare like with like.

  • How do you help Year 7s get organised: planners, homework apps, equipment checks, tutor check-ins?
  • How are homework and deadlines communicated to parents?
  • What clubs and activities run for Year 7, and how many pupils take part?
  • What instrumental lessons, ensembles and choirs are there, and does GCSE Music run every year?
  • Which languages are taught from Year 7, and can pupils take two?

9. Talking it through with Sam

  • Ask for their view of each school before you share yours. Once they know what you think, you’re hearing your own opinion back.
  • Ask what they noticed at each school (the people, the feel, the lunch queue), not just which they liked best.
  • Try a premortem together: “Imagine it’s Year 8 and it hasn’t gone well. What went wrong?” Do it for each of your top choices. It surfaces worries that “which did you like?” never does.
  • Ask: “Where could you see yourself having a good day? Where would a bad day be easier to handle?”
  • Their view matters even if you weigh things differently. Children who feel they had a say tend to settle better.
  • Whatever the outcome in March, children adapt. Most children who are anxious in July are settled by October half-term.

How this report works

The “describe your child” statements are everyday versions of well-researched temperament and personality dimensions. They are not a psychological assessment and have not been validated as a scale; they are a structured way to think about fit. Each school’s match combines those needs with your priorities and the published data, weighting the things that matter most for your child. Where data is missing the match is pulled towards “mixed” rather than guessed.

Your visit impressions are deliberately not part of the match. Counting them would hand your own view back to you with the tool’s authority. Instead they are set beside the evidence so you can see where the two agree and where they don’t. The visit checklist asks for specific observations before an overall verdict, because one striking feature on an open evening can colour everything else (the “halo effect”).

The research behind it
Children differ in stable, measurable ways (temperament) that shape how they experience school. Parent ratings of these traits predict school adjustment and attainment.
Shiner & Caspi (2003), J. Child Psychology & Psychiatry 44(1) · Rothbart (2007), Current Directions in Psychological Science 16(4) · Poropat (2014), Learning & Individual Differences 34
Personality becomes steadily more stable with age but is only moderately stable at 11. Early adolescence brings a typical, mostly temporary, dip in conscientiousness and rise in negative emotionality.
Roberts & DelVecchio (2000), Psychological Bulletin 126(1) · Soto et al. (2011), J. Personality & Social Psychology 100(2) · Soto & Tackett (2015), Current Directions in Psychological Science 24(5)
Young adolescents do best when the school environment matches their developmental needs, especially for supportive relationships with teachers and a growing say in their own learning. A mismatch is linked to drops in motivation after the move to secondary school.
Eccles et al. (1993), “Stage–environment fit”, American Psychologist 48(2)
Motivation is strongest when three needs are met: feeling capable (competence), connected (relatedness) and having some choice (autonomy).
Ryan & Deci (2000), American Psychologist 55(1)
Self-organisation and persistence are among the strongest everyday predictors of school success. Explicit routines and organisational support help most where they are still developing.
Moffitt et al. (2011), PNAS 108(7) · Poropat (2009), Psychological Bulletin 135(2)
Some children are more strongly affected by their environment, for better and for worse. They are more affected by calm, warm settings, and also by chaotic ones.
Pluess (2015), Child Development Perspectives 9(3)
In UK studies of the move to secondary school, the strongest predictors of a difficult transition were prior anxiety, being bullied, and low school connectedness. Making new friends, settling into routines and feeling that school “gets” you predicted a good one.
Evangelou et al. (2008), DCSF Research Report RR019 · Rice, Frederickson & Seymour (2011), British J. Educational Psychology 81(2) · Rice et al. (2021), School Mental Health 13
Impressions from a single visit are vulnerable to the halo effect: one striking feature colours everything. Judgements are more reliable when broken into specific, independent assessments with the overall verdict made last, and when people judge independently before comparing notes.
Kahneman (2011), Thinking, Fast and Slow · Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein (2021), Noise · Dawes (1979), American Psychologist 34(7)
Headline results mostly reflect a school’s intake. Differences in how much a school adds for an individual child are real but smaller and less certain than league tables suggest, so fit and practicalities matter too.
Leckie & Goldstein (2009), J. Royal Statistical Society A 172(4) · Burgess et al. (2015), “What parents want”, Economic Journal 125
